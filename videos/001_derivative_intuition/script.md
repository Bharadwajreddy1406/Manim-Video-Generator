# Why the Derivative Is Instantaneous Slope

Imagine driving along a winding road.

Over ten seconds, your average speed is the distance traveled divided by ten seconds. But your speedometer answers a sharper question: how fast are you moving right now?

A graph has the same distinction.

Choose two points on a curve. The line through them is a secant line, and its slope measures the average rate of change between those points:

change in y, divided by change in x.

Now keep the first point fixed and slide the second point closer.

The interval shrinks. The average slope describes a smaller and smaller piece of the curve. And the secant line turns toward one limiting position: the tangent line.

We cannot simply put the two points in exactly the same place. Then both changes would be zero, giving zero divided by zero.

Instead, the derivative uses a limit. It asks what the secant slopes approach as the gap approaches zero.

If those slopes settle to one value, that value is the derivative. It captures the curve's rate of change at that exact input—the slope at an instant.

So instantaneous slope is not measured across a tiny fixed interval. It is the limit of average slopes as the interval vanishes.
